A TOXIC PARADISE

Venus is the second planet from the Sun and is Earth's closest planetary neighbor. It's often called Earth's twin because it's similar in size and density. These are not identical twins, however – there are radical differences between the two worlds. Venus has a thick, toxic atmosphere filled with carbon dioxide and it’s perpetually enveloped in thick, yellowish clouds of sulfuric acid that trap heat, causing a runaway greenhouse effect.

THE HOTTEST WORLD

Even though Mercury is closer to the Sun, Venus is the hottest planet in our solar system. Its thick atmosphere traps heat in a runaway greenhouse effect, making it hot enough to melt lead. Surface temperatures are about 900 degrees Fahrenheit (475 degrees Celsius). The surface is a rusty-colored landscape peppered with intense mountains and thousands of large volcanoes.

RETROGRADE ROTATION

Venus is one of only two planets that rotate from east to west. Only Venus and Uranus have this "backwards" rotation. It rotates very slowly on its axis—one day on Venus lasts 243 Earth days. The planet orbits the Sun even faster than it rotates, which means a year on Venus (225 Earth days) is shorter than a day on Venus.

HISTORY OF OBSERVATION

Named after the Roman goddess of love and beauty, Venus is the second brightest natural object in the night sky after the Moon. It can cast shadows and can be, on rare occasions, visible to the naked eye in broad daylight. Because it is closer to the Sun than Earth, it always appears to be close to the Sun, either as a "morning star" or "evening star."

SURVIVING THE PRESSURE

Exploring Venus is a massive challenge. The atmospheric pressure at the surface is 90 times that of Earth—similar to the pressure a mile below the ocean's surface. Only a few spacecraft have ever reached the surface. The Soviet Union's Venera series of landers in the 1970s and 80s were the only ones to successfully transmit data from the surface, surviving for only a few hours before the intense heat and pressure destroyed them.

NASA's Magellan mission in the 1990s used radar to map 98 percent of the surface, revealing a world dominated by volcanic features, vast plains, and high mountain ranges. Future missions like DAVINCI and VERITAS are planned for the 2030s to dive back into the Venusian atmosphere and study its geological history in unprecedented detail.
