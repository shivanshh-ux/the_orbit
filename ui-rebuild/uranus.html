THE ICE GIANT

Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun, and has the third-largest diameter in our solar system. It is one of the two "ice giants" (the other being Neptune). Most of its mass is a hot, dense fluid of "icy" materials – water, methane, and ammonia – above a small rocky core. Its atmosphere is made mostly of molecular hydrogen and atomic helium, with a small amount of methane.

SPINNING ON ITS SIDE

Uranus is unique among the planets because it is tilted so far that it essentially orbits the Sun on its side, with the axis of its spin nearly pointing at the Sun. This unusual orientation might be due to a collision with an Earth-sized object long ago. This tilt causes the most extreme seasons in the solar system: for nearly a quarter of each Uranian year, the Sun shines directly over each pole, plunging the other half of the planet into a 21-year-long, dark winter.

A PALE BLUE WORLD

Uranus gets its signature cyan-blue color from methane gas in its atmosphere. Methane absorbs the red portion of sunlight, resulting in the beautiful blue-green color we see. Unlike Jupiter and Saturn, Uranus has a mostly featureless appearance in visible light, though infrared observations reveal complex cloud structures and some of the fastest winds in the solar system.

THE RING SYSTEM

Uranus has two sets of rings. The inner system of nine rings consists mostly of narrow, dark grey rings. The outer system has two distant rings: the innermost is reddish like dusty rings elsewhere in the solar system, and the outermost is blue like Saturn's E ring. The rings were discovered in 1977 when astronomers were watching a star pass behind the planet, noticing the star blinked on and off before and after being blocked by the planet itself.

VOYAGER 2 ENCOUNTER

Only one spacecraft has ever visited Uranus. NASA's Voyager 2 flew by the planet in January 1986, coming within 50,600 miles (81,500 kilometers) of the cloud tops. Voyager 2 discovered 10 new moons, studied the planet's unique atmosphere and magnetic field, and provided the first close-up images of its ring system. Since then, astronomers have used the Hubble Space Telescope and large ground-based telescopes to continue studying this distant world.

A mission to Uranus has been identified as a high priority for the next decade of space exploration. Scientists hope to send a dedicated orbiter and atmospheric probe to understand the origin and evolution of ice giants, which appear to be common throughout our galaxy.
